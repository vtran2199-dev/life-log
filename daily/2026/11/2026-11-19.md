### A dictation breakthrough without building anything

Victor had been contemplating a Mac version of his Windows **WhisperKey** tool, but found that **Granola Voice Typing** already did something he badly wanted. Granola recognized **Button 6 on his ELECOM HUGE trackball directly**. He configured the same button to start and stop hands-free dictation, kept **Fn+Space** as a laptop-only fallback, and freed a redundant keyboard macro. This achieved the practical goal without writing or porting code.

The real test was hilarious: he spoke, left extended silent gaps over roughly **three minutes**, then clicked to stop. The full transcript appeared in **about half a second or less**, much faster than he expected. He was delighted that the recording did not terminate during thinking pauses. Later, two Granola recordings initially failed before a retry worked, and he learned to find prior dictation in **Granola's Voice Typing history** rather than assuming it was only on the clipboard. The precise internal streaming or silence-detection implementation was not established.

He compared the experience with local WhisperKey, Parakeet and Wispr Flow. The takeaway was pragmatic: **use the tool that already works** rather than undertake a macOS port just because he can. There was **no verified GitHub commit or deployment** associated with this discovery.

### A workstation that wants to become a cockpit

The Sparta **16-inch M5 Pro MacBook Pro** was already connected to a remarkably elaborate setup: two **49-inch Samsung ultrawides**, Kinesis Advantage2/Dvorak keyboard, ELECOM HUGE trackball, webcam, Jabra headset and the existing **LIONWEI dual-USB-C hub**. A **27-inch monitor was expected November 20**, not delivered Thursday.

Isaac had indicated that buying a reasonable dock was fine, but Victor was not enthusiastic about a **$300 dock or expensive monitor arm**. He investigated cheaper Ethernet-capable docks, USB switching, a keyboard tray large enough for the tall Kinesis and trackball, and a standing desk. The **broken standing desk and back pain** made ergonomics more urgent than another premium accessory. His kneeling chair sat too high and had a loose/cracked backrest; he wanted a normal chair with firm, continuous lumbar contact and useful recline. Instagram seemed to respond by flooding him with chair ads, including **Hinomi** and adjustable-backrest models. These were comparisons and shopping research, **not confirmed purchases**.

Victor also wondered why companies insist on laptops when a **Mac mini or compact desktop box** could drive a fixed desk full of monitors. The laptop still wins for coffee shops and mobility, but he barely needs its own screen when docked. He researched the M5 Pro's external-display capabilities and the relative roles of his powerful Windows CUDA machine, the work MacBook and a hypothetical Mac Studio.

### Apple magic, and the $89,397 joke

An unexpectedly satisfying discovery was **iPhone Mirroring**: Victor could control **Clash of Clans** on his Mac even though the game lacked the native Mac experience he wanted. He was thrilled by how seamlessly his iPhone, MacBook and other Apple devices worked together, while still recognizing the Windows desktop's role in gaming and CUDA-based WhisperKey.

He then explored an absurd **Mac Studio M5 Ultra configuration with 512 GB unified memory and 16 TB storage**, priced around **$29,799**. He jokingly claimed to have bought one on the company card—and then three, for a supposed **$89,397** total—before clarifying that he was messing around. **No Mac Studio purchase occurred.** The fantasy was a funny counterpoint to his real search for inexpensive desk gear.

This was a preference formed from his own use, not an objective benchmark proving one model universally superior.
