## What Happened

### The cleanup crossed midnight and immediately became a storage lesson

The day began in the middle of JobCRM’s retention campaign.

A bounded Greenhouse cleanup had landed just before midnight, so September 27 opened with the next proofs: finish draining the remaining executable Greenhouse population, prove the same prevention behavior on Ashby, then work through Ashby’s much larger stale payload set without breaking identity or alignment.

The early production checkpoints were substantial. Greenhouse’s executable B0 population was driven to zero, with roughly another 14.8 MB of logical payload removed. The Ashby drain then removed **3,184 rows**, about **36,930,240 logical bytes**, across 566 batches while the alignment checks stayed clean. What remained was not simply “more of the same”: about 390 stale-looking Ashby rows were trapped behind provider-scope mismatches, and five multi-version records were protected.

The important lesson was frustrating but durable: deleting heavy rows is not the same as watching the database file immediately shrink. Ordinary PostgreSQL deletes free space for reuse inside the table; they do not necessarily return physical storage to the provider. Early measurements showed the database around **408.35 MB**, with **job_source_versions** alone around **303.46 MB** physically. Later, the new UI showed roughly **397 MiB**, with Greenhouse at 68.7 MiB, Ashby at 113 MiB, and Lever at 9.79 MiB—but the day’s argument kept returning to the same point: logical cleanup, reusable internal space, and externally visible physical shrink are three different things.

Victor had been asking the right blunt question the whole time: if the database number does not actually go down, what exactly did the cleanup accomplish?

The answer was that the retention behavior was finally becoming safe and bounded, but physical reclamation would require a later rewrite/compaction step. The decision was to **stop rather than compact blindly**.

### The problem got redefined from “old records” to “retained catalog”

Instead of continuing to chase one tail at a time, the next analysis classified what JobCRM was actually retaining: visible versus invisible, FULL versus THIN, protected versus disposable, and useful versus merely remembered.

A major audit found **14,084 normalized non-target FULL records** holding about **165.134 MB** of current logical payload:

- Ashby: 7,595
- Greenhouse: 5,723
- Lever: 766

Only eight records in that broader audited population matched the intended target role and location combination. That was the clearest evidence yet that JobCRM’s expensive FULL layer had become wildly larger than the operator’s real working set.

The emerging policy became more precise:

- keep compact provider/source identity broadly for dedupe and provenance
- keep full descriptions for jobs that are genuinely useful or protected
- demote normalized non-target records from FULL to THIN in bounded, reversible batches
- preserve anomalies and ambiguous cases instead of treating cleanup as a giant delete button
- diagnose the retained catalog before physical compaction

The new policy UI made the cleanup concrete. It showed **1,081 executable boards**; selecting 1Password revealed 46 candidate records totaling about 1.01 MiB, with the next bounded batch containing 11 rows and 255,896 bytes. That was far more operationally trustworthy than a mysterious global “clean up 165 MB” action—but Victor also immediately noticed when the interface hid the larger context and asked the most practical question: where was the goddamn THIN button?

### Targeting truth finally became persisted data

Storage cleanup could not be trustworthy until JobCRM could say, consistently and ahead of time, which records were actually relevant.

One geography failure made the weakness impossible to ignore. Stripe roles labeled **“Poland - Remote OR Romania - Remote”** appeared in a Remote US view because the matcher interpreted the standalone letters **OR** as Oregon. The same bad assumption existed in both the TypeScript and SQL classifiers.

That bug led to a better country-detection hierarchy: prefer provider-native structured country data where Ashby or Lever exposes it; map normalized country names to ISO codes; use bounded city/state fallback only when necessary; and treat unknown geography as unknown. A Remote US job should require normalized U.S. evidence, not win admission because a free-text location happened to contain a two-letter state abbreviation.

By afternoon, the A3.2 persisted-targeting cutover was complete:

- **44,135** total source records
- **43,356** normalized
- **779** intentionally unprocessed factless historical THIN rows
- **16,883 / 16,883** discovery-backed records linked authoritatively and normalized
- **2,628** Remote US
- **923** Texas
- **3,438** in the Remote US ∪ Texas union
- **2,336** in the four target role families
- **474** in the combined normal operator target
- **70** focused tests passing

That was a major transition. Role and location targeting stopped being something recomputed inconsistently at query time and became persisted truth the UI and retention system could share.

- Which provider or board is consuming storage?

The work was still exhausting. One Luna extra-high implementation session ran for roughly 25 minutes and left Victor staring at a page that did not look dramatically different despite all the underlying changes. His reaction captured the gap between architectural completion and visible payoff: are we finally done with the UI so we can get to the real 14,000-record cleanup?

A surgical follow-up put the roadmap sequence in the right order, clarified that FULL describes persistence mechanics rather than current relevance, and recorded the operator role priority as **SC/SE → GTM → AE → BDR** without turning it into ingestion policy. Commit **8762cff** closed that docs-only refinement. Later roadmap work continued to defer background refresh and large-catalog automation until the current lifecycle was actually sane.

## Things Learned

- Free space inside PostgreSQL, logical payload removed, and billed physical database size are not interchangeable metrics.
- Cleanup policy must depend on persisted targeting truth, not title/location regexes rerun differently in every layer.
- Two-letter U.S. state matching is dangerous inside international free-text locations.
- FULL is a storage state, not proof that a job is currently relevant.
- Career verdict, operational priority, application state, and exact posting identity must remain separate.
- A durable automation needs both the procedure and an explicit route telling a fresh agent where that procedure lives.

- Use normalized provider-aware geography and role families as the shared truth for UI, JobHunt, and retention.
