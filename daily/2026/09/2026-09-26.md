# September 26, 2026

## The Day in One Sentence

Victor applied to 163 jobs, then spent the rest of the day discovering that the application machine had become faster than the supply of clean jobs—and that JobCRM had quietly grown into a 43,000-record ingestion system whose biggest problem was what it kept, not how many rows PostgreSQL could handle.

## What Happened

### 163 applications, and the bottleneck moved upstream

In the early hours, Victor put the previous two days’ pace into perspective: **163 applications on September 26, after about 179 on September 25**, with bursts approaching two applications a minute. The workflow had become fast enough that filling out forms was no longer the hard part. Reusable application details, résumé routing, and skipping optional demographic questions cut friction; finding enough good, current, correctly classified jobs was taking over as the limiting factor.

That made the scale of JobCRM suddenly feel different. Roughly 35,000 source records were not 35,000 applications. They were the enormous upstream pool being searched and filtered to produce only a few hundred real opportunities. Later live snapshots put the system at **43,460 source records**, about **22,996 FULL and 20,464 THIN**, around 22,996 discoveries, 923 opportunities, and roughly 23,295 source versions, including 299 historical versions. A separate health snapshot differed by one row, but the shape of the problem was unmistakable.

The database was about **387 MiB** against a roughly 512 MiB branch limit. The **job_source_versions** table alone accounted for about **289 MiB**. The culprit was not a scary number of rows; it was full descriptions and raw provider payloads repeated in source records and historical versions. Small count and table-size queries were quick, while a cold-cache byte attribution scan that touched those large values took about 21 seconds.

The mental model changed from “How much more can I ingest?” to “What deserves to stay full?” Keep a lightweight, durable source identity for dedupe and provenance; keep full job content in a bounded working set while it is useful; preserve richer long-term records for actual opportunities. Pruning discoveries alone would not solve the heavy-payload problem.

### Greenhouse got a safer front door—and real dogfood

The day’s Greenhouse work turned that storage lesson into an ingestion strategy. The new thin-first gate checked title negatives, target role family, geography, and a 90-day freshness window before fetching a full job description. A posting that failed or remained ambiguous at those checks stayed THIN; only a positive role match that also passed geography and freshness earned a FULL fetch. The current scope stayed Greenhouse, with remote jobs requiring positive U.S. evidence and hybrid or on-site roles limited to Texas. Ashby and Lever could wait until the filters were trustworthy.

Victor tightened shared title aliases and made the company-hunt prompt easier to reuse: one editable target-count line, high-recall candidate discovery, canonical board roots, and JobCRM Add & Sync as the live verifier. He also added a “Discovered” time filter to JobHunt so newly ingested jobs could be found immediately without changing the existing role and target filters.

The larger dogfood runs showed both the promise and the tradeoffs. An **86-board run processed 2,448 postings** and surfaced 15 new jobs, 2 changed, and 2 failures. A later **20-board run processed 1,122**, found 2 new jobs, and had no failures. The new “Last hour” view surfaced the 15 discoveries from the larger run. An audit of 6,181 recent title rejects found 50 clear false negatives (about 0.8%), 318 plausible-but-ambiguous cases, and more than 5,800 correctly filtered jobs. Only a few clear misses would have made it through geography to become FULL, so the takeaway was to keep the matcher tight rather than broaden it just to make the FULL count look bigger.

A broader ATS catalog also made manual hunting look like a temporary workflow: Greenhouse alone showed thousands of feeds, with large catalogs for Lever and other providers too. If finding boards meant repeatedly asking GPT for candidates and manually running foreground syncs, the next scale problem would be the operator’s time. Background catalog discovery and durable sync moved onto the longer-term roadmap, after the filtering and storage foundations.

### JobCRM’s documentation finally got a map

The same day, the documentation system was cleaned up so each place had one job: **AGENTS.md** for operating rules and routing, **ARCHITECTURE.md** for how the system works now, **ROADMAP.md** for unfinished sequence and priorities, and GitHub Issues for backlog and acceptance details. Scoped plans, decisions, reference material, and history could live beside those rather than competing to be “the current status.”

This mattered because closeouts had become a reconciliation chore: too many overlapping documents each claimed to describe what was current. The cleanup removed the old PLAN.md and NEXT_PHASE.md once their useful material had been redistributed. A follow-up Codex audit found the cross-tool foundation sound: AGENTS.md travels with the repo, while Cursor’s .mdc files are tool-specific mechanics. The documentation work was closed rather than left as another open-ended refactor.

### Codex came back after acting broken

After repeated 401 Unauthorized and reconnecting failures the previous day, Victor tested Codex again against JobCRM. One shell start was rejected, but the next attempt worked. Codex completed read-only smoke tests: it inspected the repository and traced the application-helper route through its related code, with a clean working tree and no changes. The failures looked more like an authentication/session or service problem than a JobCRM code problem.

Cursor and Codex usage also became part of the practical planning. Cursor’s allowance was near depletion before its October 4 window ended, so Victor planned to finish the remaining Cursor work and then move to Codex, managing both pools deliberately rather than burning through either without a schedule.

### Job search still needed a human to sort the feed

The Solutions Consultant search kept serving Victor Account Executive postings several pages deep. He wondered whether LinkedIn was ignoring the keyword or whether the useful results for that search were already exhausted, and asked to research the behavior. At the same time, he worked through repeated numbered review batches and asked for full CRM payloads after selecting jobs. At this volume, exact job identity mattered: the right selection had to map to the right application record, résumé, and review evidence.

### Retention moved from theory to tiny proofs

By evening, the infrastructure sequence had sharpened: prove a real THIN→FULL reclassification first; build a read-only Database Health/Admin view next; then design and dogfood safe FULL→THIN retention; follow with a Stripe identity audit and durable background sync; only later expand ATS feed evaluation and bounded Greenhouse draining. Applications and interviews still came before infrastructure.

The first THIN→FULL proof succeeded on a Justworks listing. Later, bounded one- and two-record Greenhouse retention dogfoods showed that a source could be demoted while its provider/source identity remained available for dedupe; the heavy discovery/version content could be removed without breaking alignment checks. Victor kept the scope narrow and did not start a broad sweep.

The attempted Greenhouse cleanup then hit its safety gate. A rollback branch was mandatory, but the Neon connection lacked the project scope needed to create one. The run stopped **before mutation**: no batches, records, discoveries, or versions were deleted or demoted, and all five alignment checks stayed at zero. The failure was frustrating, but it did exactly what the safety rule was there to do. Victor then supplied the Neon project and production-branch identifiers so they could be recorded for the next attempt.

### The Life Log had to catch up with the day

The Life Log itself had already become part of the chronology problem. Its September 26 file was created at 1:14 a.m., before most of the day existed, and a same-day existing-file rule later blocked the proper closeout. The prompt was corrected so the scheduled run could reconcile today’s existing file through the run time while leaving earlier dates alone.

The first chronology repair correctly stopped treating September 25 as September 26, but it was too thin: it left out most of the actual applications, JobCRM work, and personal day. Victor called for a second-pass reconstruction using cross-chat evidence and the richer Git history. September 24 and 25 stayed where they were; this file was rebuilt around September 26 Central.

## Wins

- Applied 163 jobs while identifying clean inventory—not form completion—as the growing constraint.
- Shipped and dogfooded Greenhouse thin-first filtering, shared title aliases, and a recent-discovery view.
- Confirmed that a bounded source can move between THIN and FULL while preserving identity, then stopped cleanup when rollback setup was unavailable.
- Finished a practical documentation ownership model and verified the repo remained usable from Codex.
- Reached Town Hall 11 and found a use for Dark Elixir that had been sitting at its cap.

## Friction / Lows

- Tens of thousands of source records and full historical payloads were crowding a small database plan; one heavy storage-attribution query took about 21 seconds cold.
- LinkedIn’s Solutions Consultant results wandered into AE territory, and high-volume numbered review batches made exact application tracking essential.
- The Greenhouse cleanup could not proceed without a rollback branch, and Neon lacked the project scope needed to create it.
- The Life Log’s early entry and same-day conflict handling both failed to capture the day properly.

## Things Learned

- PostgreSQL row count was not the storage emergency; repeated descriptions and raw payloads in the source-version table were.
- A strict title/geography/freshness gate can keep whole-company feeds mostly THIN without necessarily hiding worthwhile jobs.
- Recent-discovery visibility matters when the normal workflow drains jobs by provider and role rather than by arrival time.
- A rollback prerequisite that cannot be satisfied should halt cleanup before the first mutation.
