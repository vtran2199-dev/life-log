# September 25, 2026

## The Day in One Sentence

JobCRM stopped behaving like a small personal CRM and started behaving like a real ingestion system: Victor pushed ATS sourcing, review, and application throughput hard enough to expose failures in Greenhouse, geography filtering, résumé routing, application UX, and the underlying data model—all while the application workflow itself became almost absurdly fast.

## What Happened

### The bottleneck moved upstream

The day was no longer mainly about learning how to submit applications faster. That part was already becoming solved.

The real problem became inventory: getting enough legitimate, relevant jobs into JobCRM, filtering them correctly, reviewing them quickly, and putting the right résumé in front of Victor without wasting time on stale, foreign, duplicate, or misclassified roles.

Earlier application dogfooding had already shown how much small pieces of friction mattered. On Ashby, basics like phone and location could still require manual correction even when the form looked filled. The extension work became increasingly practical rather than theoretical: open the helper where it was useful, keep the workflow deterministic, and remove UI that slowed down the actual application path.

### Lever became a real ingestion lane

Lever moved from “next ATS to try” into the actual JobCRM ingestion architecture. The goal stayed bounded: reuse the provider-neutral pipeline instead of inventing an entirely separate product around Lever.

The important shift was conceptual. Ashby, Greenhouse, and Lever were no longer three unrelated websites. They were three providers feeding the same downstream system:

source → ingest → normalize → dedupe → JobHunt filter → GPT review → Apply Queue → human application.

That model made the upstream system much more powerful—but it also meant provider-scale mistakes could now flood the same database and UI.

### Greenhouse was where scale finally fought back

The large Greenhouse sync was the most dramatic proof.

A 95-board run completed all 95 boards in about 25 minutes and reported:

- 3,626 jobs processed
- 2,168 new
- 1 changed
- 2 unchanged
- 45 failed

The failures were not one bug. They represented several different system-level failure modes:

- Greenhouse HTTP 404s
- operations aborted by timeout
- responses too large for the configured limit
- strong source identity resolving to conflicting existing records

That was the point where “some ATS boards failed” stopped being an adequate explanation. The system had become large enough that transport failures, provider behavior, payload size, identity rules, and database persistence all mattered separately.

### Freshness became a real data-quality problem

The source feed was also capable of returning technically reachable jobs that were useless in practice.

One of the clearest examples was an Account Executive opening that was still reachable even though the source age was measured in years. The lesson was simple: a job being discoverable or technically live did not mean it belonged in the working application pool.

The system needed a stronger distinction among:

- discovered
- live
- fresh
- review-worthy
- worth applying to

That pushed freshness and liveness from nice-to-have metadata into actual eligibility rules.

### “Remote” turned out not to mean “Remote US”

The filtering layer then exposed a second class of bad inventory.

The supposedly Remote US view surfaced jobs in places like Portugal, France, and New Zealand. That made it obvious that location matching could not be fail-open around vague values like “Remote.” If a job could not be positively identified as eligible for the U.S. target geography, it should not quietly leak into the primary queue.

This was one of those bugs that looked ridiculous in the UI but was actually architectural: the system had been treating absence of evidence as evidence of eligibility.

### Résumé routing broke in the most obvious possible way

Another painful dogfood failure appeared in résumé recommendations.

Blatant SDR / BDR titles were being routed to the Account Executive résumé because the advisor was over-weighting company language, technical vocabulary, or adjacent responsibilities instead of the strongest signal sitting in plain sight: the job title and the actual core motion.

That was especially alarming because résumé routing is supposed to remove thinking from a high-throughput workflow, not introduce a silent category error into hundreds of applications.

The emerging rule became much sharper: title and core duties must dominate résumé-lane classification. Fancy wording should not turn an SDR job into an AE, SC, or GTM role.

### Greenhouse application UX got simpler by removing UI

The Greenhouse helper also hit a dogfood-specific failure: the injected in-page helper could interfere with MyGreenhouse modal behavior by acting like an outside click.

Instead of forcing the old pattern to survive, the architecture was simplified.

Greenhouse/MyGreenhouse application pages intentionally stopped getting an injected helper. The extension toolbar would open or focus a separate `/application-helper` surface instead. Ashby could keep the in-page helper where it actually worked.

This was a good example of the day’s broader theme: production dogfooding kept deleting assumptions that seemed reasonable in a smaller test.

### The application machine became ridiculously fast

The strangest part was that all of this building was happening while Victor was still applying at a rate that would have seemed absurd only days earlier.

In the work session that ran into the next calendar day, Victor described the just-finished application total as **163 jobs**. The exact midnight boundary of that count was discussed after the fact, so the number is best understood as the throughput of the September 25 work session rather than a perfectly timestamped calendar counter.

The speed came from accumulated workflow improvements rather than one trick: preselected résumés, ATS-specific helpers, faster review packets, deterministic routing, saved MyGreenhouse profile data, and increasingly aggressive skipping of optional demographic/EEO sections when they were not required.

At burst speed, the actual act of applying could approach roughly two jobs per minute.

The bottleneck was no longer clicking Apply.

It was supplying the machine with enough clean jobs.

## Things Learned

- Thousands of jobs are easy to ingest; tens of thousands of poorly bounded source records are a different operational regime.
- “Remote” is not a geography policy.
- A reachable ATS posting is not necessarily fresh or useful.
- Provider identity and canonical identity are separate concerns and can conflict.
- Résumé routing needs stronger deterministic anchors than general semantic similarity.
- The fastest application UI may be the one with less injected UI, not more.
- Dogfooding at real throughput reveals failures that small tests simply do not expose.

## Funny / Memorable Moments

- A Remote US filter managed to produce New Zealand.
- The résumé router looked at jobs literally titled SDR/BDR and somehow decided “Account Executive.”
- Greenhouse went from “this should be another structured provider” to a 95-board stress test with 45 failures in one run.
- The more the application workflow improved, the less the actual application form mattered; the system kept moving the bottleneck backward until inventory and infrastructure became the problem.

## Open Loops

- Understand and fix the Greenhouse failure classes instead of treating them as one generic sync error.
- Tighten U.S. geography eligibility so ambiguous remote locations fail closed.
- Make title/core duties the strongest résumé-routing signal.
- Keep simplifying the Greenhouse application path around the separate application-helper surface.
- Inspect how much data the ingestion architecture is retaining and whether the persistence model is still appropriate at this scale.
