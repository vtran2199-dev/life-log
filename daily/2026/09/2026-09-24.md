# September 24, 2026

## The Day in One Sentence

A completely ordinary job-search day somehow turned into a high-throughput recruiting operating system, an ATS research project, an extension debugging session, an email triage problem, and finally the birth of a repository designed to remember the whole thing so the chats themselves no longer have to.

## What Happened

### The application machine went from “system” to absurd

JobCRM crossed into a different scale today. At one point the Command Center showed 102 applications for the day, 182 over the last seven days, 317 over the last 30 days, 286 awaiting response, four in process, and one offer. Later in the day, Victor described the total application count for the day as roughly 179.

The part that felt strange was not merely the volume. The repetitive work had somehow become engaging because the system around it was still being built. Applying to jobs — normally one of the most tedious workflows imaginable — started to feel like a video game loop: source, review, apply, observe friction, improve the tooling, repeat.

The contrast was ridiculous. A few days earlier the database had been hundreds of jobs; by today Victor was talking about roughly 10,000 jobs in the system and being unable to manually keep up with the available inventory.

### Greenhouse finally lost its application-channel privileges

Greenhouse friction became impossible to ignore. Today’s applications included companies such as PagerDuty, 7shifts, Automattic, Luma Health, Solo.io, Orkes, OpenSesame, CoreView, Cresta, Tekmetric, and Upshop, while repeated email/security verification events continued to interrupt the workflow.

A read-only audit of the Throughput & Friction data showed that Greenhouse applications could still succeed after verification friction, so it was not a total outage. Eon eventually reached a successful “Thank you for applying” state after testing. But the broader conclusion held: Greenhouse was recurring intermittent friction, not a clean high-volume lane.

The decision was to stop treating Greenhouse as the preferred place to hammer applications. Keep Greenhouse useful for discovery/inventory, preserve the database, but shift application energy toward Ashby and eventually Lever.

### The ingestion architecture became clearer

A major realization landed around ATS ingestion.

Ashby can often provide a whole-board payload cheaply enough that JobCRM can ingest broadly and keep thin records for poor-fit jobs. Greenhouse is different: pulling every full description up front is wasteful, especially on giant company boards. The cleaner Greenhouse pattern is to list cheaply, filter first, and hydrate only survivors.

Lever should eventually get its own adapter rather than being forced into either the Ashby or Greenhouse model.

That reframed the current bottleneck. The problem was no longer simply “find more jobs.” The problem was upstream relevance, dedupe, and deciding how much data each ATS deserves before a job earns deeper processing.

There was also a bigger automation thought: if Postgres/Neon can become the first dedupe and ingestion layer, public ATS sources could potentially flow into the database on a schedule instead of repeatedly sending giant candidate lists through GPT just to discover what is new.

### Ashby became the cleaner hunting ground

A large part of the day went into finding and testing canonical public Ashby board roots.

Multiple company-hunt batches were run with increasingly large targets. The early batches were productive; later batches started producing far fewer genuinely new companies as the easy surface area became saturated. That led to a strategy change: instead of endlessly asking GPT to discover another 50 or 80 boards while doing increasingly painful dedupe, research should move toward finding a durable Ashby directory/source strategy and letting Postgres become the hard dedupe boundary.

That is a much more scalable problem than “please magically remember every board we have ever seen.”

### JobCRM kept getting repaired while it was being used

Several concrete JobCRM changes landed during the day.

The Apply Queue was changed to use the Recommended résumé, with edits persisting separately from lane and `resumeUsed`. A tracked-résumé fix followed so new unapplied tracking uses the recommended résumé before falling back to the lane. Focused tests, production builds, and diff checks passed.

The résumé-routing/review prompt was also exercised through fresh JobHunt batches. That surfaced a calibration issue: an outbound SDR role at Hightouch had been classified as “SC + GTM Hybrid,” which was too clever for its own good. The role was fundamentally outbound SDR and should have been routed to the BDR résumé. That became another reminder that the reviewer should classify based on actual ownership of the work, not the presence of technical-sounding words.

### The Ashby application helper got more serious

The browser extension work moved forward too.

A tested slice made detail and portfolio buttons deterministic clipboard-copy controls, kept `Fill Basics` as the only direct DOM-writing action, tightened Ashby page detection to bounded `/application` paths with visible supported controls, added conservative current-company matching, excluded hidden controls, and kept website/portfolio autofill disabled. The extension suite passed 33 tests and the Greenhouse tests stayed green.

Then another problem appeared: the helper could claim a field was successfully filled because the DOM property temporarily contained the new value, even though Ashby/React’s controlled state had not actually accepted it. A later render could restore the empty value. The root cause became clear: verifying `element.value` immediately after dispatching `input`/`change` proves only browser DOM state, not framework acceptance.

In other words: the extension could say “success” while the ATS quietly disagreed.

### OpenClaw moved from curiosity into the operating-system backlog

OpenClaw remained a looming rabbit hole, but it finally received a clearer place in the system rather than existing as another thing to keep mentally open.

The priority order became: fix Greenhouse ingestion, build Lever later, and prototype OpenClaw as part of the application workflow rather than letting it derail the current system. Tasks were captured, and a Notion notebook named “Open Claw” was created as the durable place for ongoing learning.

### The inbox started fighting back

After the application volume, email became its own operational problem.

Victor manually went through roughly 140 application-related emails, mostly scanning subjects for anything that did not look like a generic “Thank you for applying.” The unusual one was from Clipboard for the Sales Operations Analyst role: instead of a rejection or receipt, it invited him to a short take-home assessment.

For a moment this looked like unexpectedly surviving another gate. Then the suspicion set in that the assessment might simply be a broad automated screening step sent to many applicants. Either way, it was exactly the kind of signal that was easy to lose inside the flood of application notifications.

That pushed the idea of routing job-search email into a dedicated Gmail-style inbox/forwarding setup so the main inboxes do not become unusable.

### The life-log idea finally became infrastructure

Late at night, the problem shifted from losing job information to losing the day itself.

There had been so many disposable chats that even a very good conversation about the logging system was nearly lost among them. That led to the key idea: ChatGPT cross-chat retrieval does not need to become the permanent archive. It only needs to be good enough to reconstruct each day once.

From there, the architecture became obvious:

raw chats and real life → daily Markdown → weekly synthesis → monthly synthesis → yearly history.

Daily files would become the canonical historical snapshots. Weekly summaries would reconcile repeated storylines and hindsight without silently rewriting the daily record. Monthly and yearly summaries would compress the lower levels further.

The guiding principle became:

**Chats can be disposable because the knowledge is not.**

The repository `vtran2199-dev/life-log` was created at 11:28:10 PM America/Chicago on September 24, 2026. It was then initialized with `README.md`, `AGENTS.md`, and versioned prompts for daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly logging.

And this file became its first actual memory.

## Wins

- Pushed application throughput to a level where the bottleneck is now system architecture rather than willingness to click Apply.
- Identified Greenhouse as recurring friction rather than a total channel failure and made a concrete decision to stop prioritizing it for application volume.
- Clarified ATS-specific ingestion strategy: broad/thin Ashby ingestion, cheap-list-then-hydrate Greenhouse, dedicated Lever adapter later.
- Shipped JobCRM résumé tracking and Apply Queue improvements with tests/build checks passing.
- Advanced the Ashby application helper and found the controlled-input false-success problem instead of trusting the UI.
- Turned OpenClaw from an amorphous distraction into a tracked future workflow.
- Created `life-log`, converting disposable conversations into the input layer for a durable personal history.

## Friction / Lows

- Greenhouse security/email verification repeatedly interrupted application flow.
- Ashby board discovery started hitting diminishing returns as the easy unique companies were exhausted.
- Application volume created an inbox flood large enough that useful signals could disappear inside routine ATS receipts.
- The Ashby helper exposed a subtle controlled-state problem: apparent DOM success did not guarantee ATS/framework success.
- The number of parallel chats was high enough that an important conversation about preserving knowledge was nearly lost the same day it happened.

## Things Learned

- Relevance retrieval and chronology are different problems. Cross-chat memory can reconstruct a day surprisingly well, but the durable archive needs explicit calendar boundaries and dedupe against prior daily files.
- Postgres is a much better hard dedupe boundary than asking an LLM to remember every company/source ever encountered.
- Different ATS providers deserve different ingestion strategies; forcing them into one universal adapter wastes work.
- A DOM value check is not sufficient evidence that a React-controlled form accepted an input.
- High-volume job applying can become oddly motivating when every repetitive action feeds improvements back into the system.

## Decisions

- Stop prioritizing Greenhouse for application volume; keep it for discovery/inventory.
- Continue leaning into Ashby and build Lever support later.
- Keep JobCRM as the authoritative dedupe/state layer rather than trying to make GPT perfectly remember source history.
- Keep OpenClaw in the backlog/workflow instead of letting it interrupt higher-priority ATS work.
- Use GitHub, not Notion, as the canonical archive for daily life logs.
- Keep the filesystem stable: `daily/YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD.md`, then weekly, monthly, and yearly rollups.
- Preserve daily entries as historical snapshots; let higher-level summaries add hindsight instead of rewriting the past.
