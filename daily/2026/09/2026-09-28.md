# September 28, 2026

## The Day in One Sentence

September 28 was the day Victor’s job search became legible as a system rather than a pile of applications: he completed an encouraging UiPath recruiter screen, reopened a strong ERP-sales path with N’ware, booked another recruiter call, audited the first 80 resolved outcomes from 686 applications, pushed JobCRM toward a complete read-only agent interface, and kept building a criteria-first Greenhouse sourcing workflow—while also realizing that the Life Log had effectively removed “journaling” from journaling.

## What Happened

### The archive felt magical

The day opened with Victor looking back at the September 27 Life Log and having a genuine “holy shit” reaction.

The entry had reconstructed a day he had not deliberately sat down to document. He had simply lived inside GPT, code, GitHub, Notion, TickTick, JobCRM, Vercel, and Zoho; the evidence trail was already there. The system turned that exhaust into a durable narrative.

That led to a clearer model for the whole archive:

- daily files preserve high-resolution memory
- weekly summaries turn days into a story
- monthly summaries reveal trajectory
- yearly summaries preserve the era
- conversation with the archive is likely the interface Victor will actually use

He did not imagine himself rereading every daily page like a traditional diary. That sounded mentally exhausting. The more natural behavior was to ask the archive questions: when did this problem start, what was I thinking, was I actually progressing, and how did my interpretation change?

The phrase that captured the feeling was simple: **the day survived.**

### TickTick became the visible control layer

Victor described the physical reality of his setup: TickTick stays open essentially all day on the upper monitor above two 49-inch displays. JobCRM is the main workspace; Vercel and Zoho are usually nearby; Notion is opened more selectively for readable personal and project context.

The task system was already life-changing, but daily use still felt confusing. The preferred operating model remained manual and intentional rather than fully automated:

- one canonical JobCRM backlog

The recurring pattern was becoming clearer: tools were most useful when each had one job, not when every tool tried to become the whole operating system.

### LinkedIn’s data stayed locked behind its own machinery

Victor investigated LinkedIn job-search traffic through DevTools, trying to determine whether the search results could be captured more cleanly than the Gemini/OCR workflow.

He selected a Homebase Lead Revenue Operations Specialist role in Houston and watched the network stream fill with requests, but the useful job batch and detail data never appeared in readable form. Requests kept increasing; many rows looked invalid; even known strings such as Equinix and job ID 4447560283 could not be found in the inspected responses.

The traffic appeared to be encoded through LinkedIn’s SDUI/protobuf-style machinery rather than exposed as a simple JSON batch.

The resulting sourcing boundary became clearer:

- LinkedIn remains Gemini/manual because the platform and account-risk boundary are hostile
- Greenhouse and SmartRecruiters are much better candidates for criteria-first foreground capture
- ATS Feeds remains the background whole-market path

The lesson was not that LinkedIn lacked structured data. It was that extracting and automating it safely was the wrong layer to fight.

### JobHunt cleanup passed its useful tests—and exposed one real bug

A JobHunt cleanup shipped and was validated against production behavior.

The sibling model worked. One N2 representative correctly stood in for nine identical siblings, and the packet contained the representative rather than ten redundant copies. Archive and Restore also worked live.

Discard did not.

A Pure Storage discard attempt failed with:

**value.toISOString is not a function**

A later Mixpanel mixed-state test showed Austin as Archived and New York as Fresh. Discard surfaced an error/query mismatch even though the source appeared to move into Discarded state.

That produced a practical trust boundary:

- Archive/Restore was the trusted live path
- Discard had an inconsistent state/error problem
- Discarded navigation, packet preflight, and Source/Apply polish belonged in follow-up work
- documentation and GitHub issues #10–#12 recorded the gaps without pretending the broken action was ready

The test was valuable because it separated “the UI exists” from “the operation is trustworthy.”

### The first real conversion baseline replaced vibes with evidence

Later, Victor used the new application-outcomes surface to inspect what had actually happened across the job search.

The full cohort contained **686 applications**:

- 602 still unresolved or merely Applied
- 80 resolved outcomes
- 4 other statuses

Within the 80 resolved cases:

- 68 were early rejections
- 12 progressed
- resolved progression rate: **15.0%**

Progressions by lane were:

- Account Executive: 8
- BDR/SDR: 3
- Solutions Consultant: 1
- GTM Systems: 0
- Hybrid: 0

Apollo was the strongest positive SC/GTM example and, emotionally, the only one Victor really cared about. He disliked many of the other companies in that lane and did not want to launch into a broad résumé rewrite just because the raw conversion count looked bad.

The narrower audit asked the right question: were rejected SC/GTM/Hybrid jobs actually good targets, or were they roles requiring backgrounds Victor did not have?

The dated findings were added to **context/RESUME_CONVERSION_ANALYSIS.md** in the canonical job-search repo and pushed to GitHub. The audit remained descriptive rather than pretending it proved causation. Unresolved applications stayed out of the failure count. The conclusion was to preserve the evidence for a future Generation 3 decision without reopening career calibration immediately.

### JobCRM began turning into a read-only agent interface

The outcomes audit also demonstrated what the JobCRM MCP needed to become.

Victor wanted ChatGPT and Codex to answer complete operational questions:

- Why did this application fail?
- Was it a poor target or a résumé-presentation problem?
- Which résumé and generation were actually submitted?
- What did the employer require?
- What did the stored advisor say?
- What happened after application?
- Which interviews or progression events followed?

The emerging steady-state design used eight read-only tools centered on:

- opportunity search
- a complete opportunity case object
- paginated case sections when evidence is large
- application outcomes
- queue, pipeline, upcoming interviews, and engagement

Older thin tools such as get_opportunity could eventually retire once the case surface covered ordinary use. The design also made data limits explicit: exact historical JDs, offers, rejection reasons, and stage-transition history may not always be persisted.

This was a strategy/design checkpoint, not permission to mutate Neon or rush into Build. By the end of the evening, the operating split was documented in Notion:

- Codex investigates the local repository first because it can see git, shell, tests, runtime, and uncommitted work
- ChatGPT reviews the architecture and product fit before Build
- contradictions return to diagnosis
- Victor owns desired behavior and approval

That division was materially better than asking one agent to discover, design, implement, and validate everything in one opaque run.

### Greenhouse moved from company boards toward criteria-first foreground sourcing

Victor continued pushing on the sourcing bottleneck: he wanted to start from job criteria, not manually guess company boards forever.

The planned Greenhouse foreground V1 was tightly bounded:

MyGreenhouse current search page → one toolbar click → capture only the current result batch → server-side exact dedupe → skip known/applied/provider-applied jobs → cheap title/location/date filtering → hydrate survivors → canonical FULL ingest into Fresh JobHunt.

Important constraints stayed explicit:

- current page only
- manual page navigation
- no crawler
- no automatic board registration
- no Opportunity or application side effects
- do not auto-promote a discovery
- preserve foreground browser_capture separately from background ats_sync
- keep the application modal path separate because injected UI can close it

The implementation landed in commit **c3d6793 — Add Greenhouse foreground search capture**. Ninety-six targeted tests and the build passed, but browser verification had not yet been performed.

The first real click immediately proved why that distinction mattered: Victor entered Greenhouse through a bookmark, clicked the extension on the search workflow, and the extension opened Application Helper instead of the new capture action.

The intended routing was now unmistakable:

- MyGreenhouse search page → foreground capture
- individual job/application context → Application Helper

The code existed, but the user-visible entry point was not yet behaving correctly.

Victor also ran a 50-company Greenhouse hunt for AE, Solutions Consultant, GTM/RevOps, and BDR targets. It returned canonical sources including Anthropic, Apollo.io, Checkr, LaunchDarkly, PagerDuty, Tines, Eventbrite, Zip, and Remote. JobCRM Add & Sync remained the verifier.

## Things Learned

- A resolved progression rate is useful only when unresolved applications and poor-fit targets are not mislabeled as failures.
- Career verdict, application truth, process state, activity history, and next-follow-up obligation are different data.
- A read-only MCP becomes valuable when it returns a complete case with provenance, not when it merely exposes more tables.
- Browser automation should start at the human-controlled foreground boundary and preserve how the job was discovered.
- Passing unit tests and a build does not prove the extension opens the right action on the actual page.
- Personal archives become more usable when conversation is the retrieval layer and files remain the durable truth underneath.
