# October 3, 2026

## The Day in One Sentence

October 3 was the day JobCRM stopped being “just a big app” and turned into a consciously operated system: Victor rebuilt the project’s control layer, methodically took Ashby from comparative preflight through bounded dogfood to a complete 4,036-board catalog scan, learned again that backend capability without an operator path is basically a rocket ship without a start button, mapped the next ATS battlefield, shifted more deeply from Cursor into VS Code/Codex, and still had enough mental bandwidth left to poke at local AI tooling, career logistics, and several deeply weird side quests.

## What Happened

### JobCRM got a human control layer before more Ashby work

The day opened with Victor trying to solve a problem that had become increasingly obvious as JobCRM grew: the repository had plenty of truth, but a human operator still needed an obvious place to understand what was happening right now.

That led to a documentation and operating-state cleanup rather than immediately charging into more implementation.

The repo work established a clearer control surface around files such as:

- `A⭐NOW.md`
- `MAP.md`
- `ROADMAP.md`
- active / paused / completed execution-plan lifecycle rules
- documentation-impact and migration reporting rules

The cleanup was pushed in commit **47598241fa73a6527167cbe6f877d7cb16ed0011**, followed by a more explicit human-control layer in commit **9b7281de4952dde80d27aedae70a62a2e13f9062**.

The principle was simple but important: Victor should not have to reconstruct repo state from dozens of Markdown files every time he re-entered the project. The system needed a current “what matters now?” surface plus a map of where deeper truth lived.

That work also reflected a broader shift in how Victor was operating. He had spent months building increasingly complex systems, but now he was explicitly unwilling to be blind inside them.

### Cursor started giving way to VS Code in a way that felt bigger than changing editors

Victor opened `job-crm.code-workspace` and discovered Windows still wanted to launch it in Cursor.

He reassociated the workspace with VS Code.

Technically, that was trivial. Emotionally, it was not.

Cursor had been the environment where much of the earlier building habit formed, so moving deeper into VS Code/Codex felt a little like leaving an old operating environment behind. But the new setup fit the more mature version of the project: repo truth, visible plans, multiple terminals, explicit agent instructions, and a more deliberate human control layer.

Victor realized that the project had crossed a complexity threshold where “just ask the AI and hope it knows” was no longer acceptable. The environment itself needed to make state visible.

### Ashby moved from comparison to a real modernization plan

The big technical storyline was Ashby.

Victor did not want to repeat the painful Greenhouse path blindly. The goal was to inspect what Greenhouse had taught the system, reuse the good parts, and avoid copying its accidental complexity.

The comparative preflight handoff was stored and pushed in commit **23a163b354595a008535fe2da6dd90692edbe906**.

The initial rule was conservative:

**preflight → parent execution plan → implementations → dogfood → next provider**

Not:

**“Ashby exists, therefore build everything.”**

The early database baseline showed why care mattered:

- database size: roughly **190 MiB**
- source records: **52,571**
- FULL: **3,141**
- THIN: **49,430**
- Ashby registered companies: **886**
- Ashby source records: **19,084**
- Ashby FULL records: **1,951**
- Ashby heavy payload: roughly **22.8 MiB**

A live preflight against a small sample showed that normal boards could return complete listing sets cheaply, but Airwallex exceeded the 5 MiB response limit. Geography and company naming also needed calibration before a parent plan could honestly be called ready.

That was exactly the point of the preflight: discover the weirdness before designing around imaginary normality.

### Phase 2 proved the control room on a bounded sample

The next step was selected production dogfood.

Eight runnable boards were attempted.

Results:

- **8 checked**
- **0 failed**
- **0 remaining**
- **1,301 listings**

Representative semantic examples were reviewed and accepted.

The important distinction was that this did **not** authorize the catalog-wide run. Victor wanted the system to prove the operator workflow first, then separately unlock the large finite scan.

That discipline mattered because JobCRM had repeatedly demonstrated that a technically correct backend path could still be unusable in practice.

### The full Ashby catalog scan finally ran

Once the unlock was separately planned and authorized, the finite scan ran across the purchased Ashby catalog.

Final result:

- **4,036 boards checked**
- **14 persistent failures**
- **0 normal remaining**
- **61,003 listings**
- **523 Target**
- **1,456 Fallback**
- **59,024 Hidden**

Target-family distribution:

- AE: **231**
- AM: **102**
- SC: **68**
- GTM: **36**
- BDR: **86**

The fourteen remaining failures were not treated as “just retry it forever” noise:

- **13 HTTP 404**
- **1 Bjak response-size failure**

The counts reconciled cleanly, and the milestone was documented rather than left as a chat-only accomplishment.

The full-catalog closeout was pushed in commit **17f81efb2d3050fed986f0aca3b23370b6337e77**.

The scan also produced one of the clearest economic comparisons of the provider work so far. Ashby delivered approximately **0.130 Targets per board**, around **2.1× Greenhouse’s per-board target density** and roughly **3.8× its per-listing density**.

That immediately reinforced Victor’s instinct: focus on Target yield first and stop spending energy reviewing fallback just because fallback exists.

### The scanner taught another operator lesson: backend capability is not enough

The most frustrating pattern of the day was not a bad classifier or broken database.

It was something much dumber.

The system repeatedly built meaningful backend capability and then failed to expose the obvious operator control needed to use it.

Victor described it as building a **rocket ship without a start button**.

By late evening, at least five variants of the same mistake had surfaced across the Ashby work:

- scanner capability without the obvious JobHunt control
- arbitrary-board inspection without a clean operator path
- retry/recovery capability without a usable trigger
- full-catalog workflow without the obvious run control
- hydration capability without a sufficiently explicit operator gate

There was even an invalid expectation that an idempotence rerun should work against production behavior that had intentionally been disabled.

This was not merely a UI complaint. It became a design rule:

**If an operator workflow exists, the product must expose the progress, failures, controls, and current state needed to actually operate it.**

A later commit, **29a2f63**, added an operator-path preflight gate so the same class of failure would be harder to repeat.

A bounded control for single-board Ashby work landed in commit **1b79e1a74f49957996d35a11746a6f7432ee4c0f**.

### Phase 3 immediately exposed the next real blocker

After the catalog scan, Victor did not care about lovingly reviewing all 1,456 fallback findings.

He wanted Target jobs.

The recommended semantic check was only about 10–15 representative Target examples across AE, AM, SC, GTM, and BDR before moving into selective hydration.

That review exposed another structural issue: broad hydration could not safely proceed until identity aliases and conflicts were handled correctly.

So Phase 3 was not “done.” The scan was done. The next gate was clear.

This distinction mattered because Victor had become increasingly intolerant of documentation that blurred:

- code exists
- test passed
- dogfood passed
- full run passed
- hydration passed
- production workflow is actually usable

Each state now needed to mean exactly what it said.

### ATS WARS became the next sourcing map

The Ashby success immediately raised the next question: what provider comes after Ashby?

Victor researched Workday, Lever, and SmartRecruiters and saved the conclusions into a Notion page titled **ATS WARS** under the JobCRM area.

The emerging order was:

**Ashby → Lever → reconsider Workday / SmartRecruiters later**

The reasoning was practical rather than ideological.

A free Workday seed list with fewer than 1,000 boards was not compelling enough to justify building a new provider path.

Lever, by contrast, already had roughly **2,600 feeds / ~70,000 jobs** available through the existing source ecosystem and therefore had much better immediate leverage.

The rule became:

**optimize for useful target yield per unit of effort, not for the prestige of adding another ATS logo.**

### The Claude-vs-GPT question got very real

Late in the day, frustration with GPT’s engineering mistakes boiled over.

Victor seriously considered whether Claude should become the primary coding/reasoning partner because the recurring JobCRM misses—especially the operator-control failures—felt too basic relative to the sophistication of the underlying implementation.

His conclusion was not that ChatGPT had no value.

It was almost the opposite:

- Claude felt stronger for deep coding/engineering
- ChatGPT remained better as a broad general-purpose partner, especially for interviews, career reasoning, and the rest of life
- the real frustration was having to trust an AI with complex repo work and then discovering it had missed a stupidly obvious human-use edge case

That tension was one of the most emotionally salient parts of the day because JobCRM was no longer a toy project. It had become infrastructure Victor genuinely depended on.

## Things Learned

- A backend workflow is not complete until the human operator can actually trigger and observe it.
- Small dogfood can prove semantics without proving full-catalog UI behavior.
- Persistent progress must distinguish active, checked, failed, and remaining states.
- Finite provider scans should end with explicit stopping conditions rather than endless retries.
- Ashby currently offers much better target density than Greenhouse.
- Provider priority should be driven by expected useful inventory per unit of engineering effort.
- Repo complexity makes human-readable control surfaces mandatory.
- Moving from one coding environment to another can represent a deeper workflow-maturity shift, not just a preference change.
- AI coding quality is not only about whether the generated backend logic is sophisticated; missing an obvious operator action can invalidate the whole experience.

## Decisions / Direction

- Finish Ashby before opening another provider.
- Prioritize **Target** findings; fallback review is not the current goal.
- After Ashby, prioritize **Lever** ahead of Workday or SmartRecruiters.
- Do not build Workday merely because a small free seed catalog exists.
- Require operator-visible controls, progress, and failure states for future provider workflows.
- Keep repo truth explicit enough that a fresh GPT/Codex session can orient itself without rediscovering the project.
- Treat Phase 3 hydration as a separately gated milestone rather than pretending the catalog scan completed the provider.
