## What Happened

### The Multiview decision got painfully real

Tuesday, October 13 was supposed to be the first day of Victor's **Multiview SMB Growth Specialist** job in Irving. The offer and onboarding were already signed, but on Sunday he seriously reconsidered making the Houston-to-Dallas move at all. This was not a new offer or a new negotiation: the **$65,000 base**, refusal of a higher base or relocation assistance, on-site **7:30 AM–4:30 PM** schedule, and expected **80–100 outbound calls per day** were already established. What changed was how sharply he weighed the actual opportunity against its immediate costs.

His objection was more specific than disliking cold calling. **Multiview One**, a roughly **$3,500/month** managed marketing and CRM package for home-services businesses, seemed difficult to differentiate in a crowded market. A previous side-by-side look at a family member's **HighLevel** interface had raised a strong white-label hypothesis, but **no underlying vendor relationship was verified**. He distinguished Multiview's established association-advertising business from the newer One sales team and asked the uncomfortable question: does a supplied list of roofers, HVAC companies, and contractors identify *buyers*, or merely companies that will have to be cold-called? The new dedicated team's actual quota attainment remained unproven in the evidence he had.

The finances made the question urgent. He reported having **less than $50 available**, needing help from family or friends to fund relocation, and facing roughly **$1,100-plus** for a month of temporary housing, alongside transportation, groceries, and credit-card payments. His parents could provide housing and food if he stayed in Houston, so taking the job was a route to independence, not the only way to avoid immediate homelessness. He worried that a move, intensive first-week onboarding, and a rigid office schedule would also interfere with interviews for jobs he might actually prefer. At one point he estimated the role might leave about **$1,500 per month** after expenses and that he might want out within **three or four months**.

The other side was real too: Multiview was a **secured, signed offer** after a long search, whereas Cision, Curri, Reflow, Sparta, JAMS, Boom, and other prospects were still possibilities at different stages. Victor explicitly questioned whether an AE-equivalent title was worth accepting if the product, sales motion, and career fit were poor. A credible BDR or outside-sales role with something worth selling might be better than an unwanted AE title. His sentiment moved strongly toward **not going**, and an assistant advised him to stay and not book the Airbnb that night. **No confirmed formal withdrawal, recruiter notification, or cancellation was established by the end of the reviewed evidence.** The scheduled October 13 start therefore remained an unresolved, time-sensitive decision, not a completed rejection.

He made the reasoning durable in the **job-search** repository. Commit **9dc80c4** added a detailed Multiview disqualification review and strengthened the career-fit and employer-screening documents. It separated firsthand observations, interview evidence, hypotheses, and unanswered questions rather than declaring the employer or product fraudulent.

### JobCRM: a rejection-email marathon with actual answers at the end

The day's biggest data operation was not another giant ATS scan. It was trying to answer a deceptively simple question: **How many companies actually rejected the applications, and how long did employers take to respond?**

The Zoho recovery became an object lesson in fragile AI workflows. The incident account described an earlier **105-match manifest** lost after a disputed email classification, repeated searches, and a checkpoint where **1,873 message bodies had reportedly been retrieved but only 39 durably persisted**. A message represented with base64 HTML had been mishandled during the dispute. These early failure details were documented as the incident account, not all independently proven from preserved artifacts. Victor was understandably furious about repeatedly paying the effort cost of work that should already have been saved.

The eventual better approach used **four local Zoho ZIP exports**, local parsing, durable audit artifacts, and a correction pass. An initial classification found **186** confirmed rejection messages among **1,356 unique emails**. Reviewing missed classifications recovered **80 more**, yielding **266 distinct confirmed rejection emails**, with **two still uncertain**. That is an *email-message* count, not 266 separate rejected applications, and the exports were not proven to cover the entire mailbox.

The final preserved October 11 response-analysis snapshot distinguished the units:

- **950 submitted applications** in the analyzed population.
- **24 applications** with a recorded positive employer response.
- **173 applications** with a confidently matched rejection email.
- **4 applications** with both kinds of response.
- **757 applications** with neither kind recorded in the reviewed evidence.
- Average time to first recorded response: **3.95 days positive** and **3.88 days rejection**; medians **3.46** and **3.11 days** respectively.
- Of the 757 without a recorded response, **541** were more than ten days old.

The separate CRM-stage count reached **238 applications marked rejected** after a **12-application verified backfill with zero failed writes**. That stage count and the 173 email-matched applications answer different questions; they should not be conflated. Earlier recovery batches of **74** and **64** rejection activities were also reported, with the 12 verified late additions closing another gap. **Cision and Curri remained active** rather than being swept into unsafe rejection matching.

The technical lessons were almost more valuable than the counts: a broad company matcher risked false associations, retrieved data was not the same as saved data, and a surprising classifier result deserved a focused correction rather than another blind full rerun. Four **job-crm** commits preserved the work: **ecce88b** ignored private recovery artifacts, **1090cd8** and **199ed3e** recorded the historical response analysis, and **3aff822** documented the failure postmortem. The private email exports and audit details were kept out of Git.

## Things Learned

- **A prospect list is not a buyer-intent list.** Cold-call volume does not establish that a product is compelling or that customers are ready to buy.
- A job title, an advertised OTE, and a signed offer are different from a role that is financially and professionally sustainable.
- **266 rejection emails**, **173 confidently matched applications**, and **238 CRM rejection-stage records** are different units; meaningful analysis depends on keeping them separate.
- **Read-back verification beats a green tool response.** The same principle applies to Zoho checkpoints, GitHub commits, and Vercel deployments.
- When repeated micro-optimizations fail, switch layers: use native bulk export, verify a small persisted sample, and resume from durable checkpoints.
- The best planner or widget is often the one that answers the next real-world action, not the one with the most controls.
